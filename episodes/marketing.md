Studio approval arrives just before recording, turning one fear into another: now the marketing has to begin.

Ben reflects on the difference between being hired for skills someone already values and selling something he created because he believes it matters. The episode moves through YouTube Symphony memories, early freelancing, family testing, app approval, and the obligation that starts when people decide to pay.
Marketing becomes less about promotion than exposure: putting violin, parenting, software, AI, and judgment in front of other people and letting them decide what it is worth.

**Keywords**: marketing, app launch, creative risk, AI products, family and work, YY Method, human judgment

<p>I was about to record an episode called “Marketing.”</p>
<p>I had the whole thing in my head because Studio still hadn&#39;t approved my three apps, and I was scared. Then, just before I recorded this, they approved all three.</p>
<p>So that changed the episode. A little.</p>
<p>Because one of the things I was scared about is gone. I&#39;m allowed to do this now. Which means the other thing I&#39;ve been scared about is suddenly right in front of me.</p>
<p>I actually have to market them.</p>
<p>I am not a marketer. I never have been. Or at least, I&#39;ve never tried marketing myself.</p>
<p>Well, that&#39;s also not true.</p>
<p>I marketed myself for years as a freelancer, but somebody else already valued the project. They had something they wanted built, and I had the skills to build it. They had the value. I had the skills.</p>
<p>Turns out, I&#39;ve never actually tried to sell something that I value without knowing whether anybody else does. And that&#39;s the part that scares me about marketing.</p>
<p>In 2009, I had the opportunity to audition for the first YouTube Symphony Orchestra. When the results came out, I started hearing about everybody else getting in. People were celebrating online, and I didn&#39;t get the congratulations email.</p>
<p>I felt terrible because I believed I was going to get in. I&#39;d put my best playing out there. I already had dozens and dozens of violin videos on YouTube. I saw musicians getting accepted who I thought were excellent, and I felt like I was right there with them. Why wasn&#39;t I chosen?</p>
<p>Eventually, I checked my spam folder.</p>
<p>Oh.</p>
<p>Duh.</p>
<p>I got in.</p>
<p>And suddenly all of that anxiety disappeared.</p>
<p>That&#39;s almost exactly what just happened again. I&#39;ve spent hundreds of hours working on these apps. I&#39;ve tested them with my family. I&#39;ve gotten other beta testers. I&#39;ve made the marketing pages. I&#39;ve submitted everything to Studio. And then I waited.</p>
<p>It&#39;s about five days before launch, and I still didn&#39;t have approval. Until just now.</p>
<p>Now I do.</p>
<p>So that uncertainty is gone. But there&#39;s another one right behind it.</p>
<p>When I first started putting violin videos on YouTube, I put my violin self in front of a camera and let the world judge me. And they did. Kind of harshly at first.</p>
<p>Not pointing any fingers. My video skills were pretty bad. I didn&#39;t even look at the camera when I first started.</p>
<p>But I hit Upload anyway.</p>
<p>The thing is, it didn&#39;t cost me anything to do that other than time, and it didn&#39;t cost anybody else anything to watch my videos other than time. So naturally, as more people watched, I just kept making more videos.</p>
<p>Freelancing introduced money, but even that was different. I didn&#39;t have a computer science degree, so people didn&#39;t have much proof that I could do what I said I could do. It took months before anybody would entertain the idea of hiring me.</p>
<p>In fact, sometimes I had to learn how to do the job in order to win the job. I literally learned some of that stuff twenty-four hours before putting in the bid.</p>
<p>Eventually, that turned out heavily in my favor. That path got me all the way to where I am now.</p>
<p>So how is marketing different?</p>
<p>This time, I have to ask somebody to pay for something that I decided was valuable. Something I created.</p>
<p>And these apps mix together things that are very close to me: violin, parenting, software, AI, and my own judgment. I&#39;ve built them the way I think they should work. I&#39;ve removed things I don&#39;t trust. I&#39;ve tested them, changed them, broken them, fixed them, and changed my mind when something didn&#39;t feel right.</p>
<p>Now I have to put them in front of other people and let them decide whether any of that is worth buying.</p>
<p>That&#39;s scary.</p>
<p>In my head, I can see two sort of possibilities. Of course there are many, but two main ones.</p>
<p>One is that nobody sees enough value. Maybe nobody buys them. Maybe I keep trying for a while, eventually decide I&#39;ve learned what I needed to learn, and let them be.</p>
<p>The other is that people actually do see value. And then I have a completely different problem, because now people are paying me. Now I have an obligation to keep improving what I&#39;ve made, listen to the people using it, and figure out what comes next.</p>
<p>I&#39;m also making a bet that if these apps become more popular, my family, particularly my kids, will become more interested and want to participate. They&#39;re getting old enough that they can help me. So potentially, I&#39;m not alone.</p>
<p>And YY &amp; Me has taught me quite a bit since I started this podcast. I know that doing something repeatedly doesn&#39;t mean I&#39;m doing it correctly. I know that I can execute something properly and still have it land wrong. I know to listen when somebody else notices something I don&#39;t, and I know that every hour I spend comes from somewhere else.</p>
<p>I&#39;ve also learned that getting help doesn&#39;t mean giving up authorship. I can use AI, ask experts, listen to my family, and listen to the people who actually use these things. But eventually I have to decide. That&#39;s the part I can&#39;t outsource.</p>
<p>That&#39;s really what the YY Method has become for me. Why? Why not? And eventually, commit.</p>
<p>So I think I&#39;m ready. Not ready in the sense that I know how to market these apps, because I don&#39;t. I&#39;ve never done this before. But I think I have enough equipment to start. I have the apps, evidence from actually using them, people who can help me, AI to help me execute, and a way to think about what happens when I&#39;m wrong.</p>
<p>The image that keeps coming into my head is jumping out of an airplane. I have the parachute. I have the goggles. I&#39;ve done what I can to get ready.</p>
<p>The unfortunate part is that I&#39;m terrified of heights.</p>
<p>And a few minutes ago, I didn&#39;t even know whether Studio was going to let me onto the plane. I was literally preparing to record this episode about what it felt like to still be waiting when the approval came in.</p>
<p>All three apps.</p>
<p>Approved.</p>
<p>So that part is over. I know I&#39;m allowed to do this now.</p>
<p>Which means I can&#39;t hide behind waiting for approval anymore.</p>
<p>The door is open.</p>
<p>I have to jump.</p>
